      How to hunt
      1. Follow the power. Black lead on battery −, circuit on. Move red down the line. Where 12 V turns into 0 V, the open is between those two points. A meter draws almost no current, so 12 V doesn't prove a wire can carry a load: confirm with the circuit on.
      2. Voltage drop. Put red and black on both ends of one wire, connector or switch with the circuit on. Rough guide with normal current flowing: about 0.1 V per connection, 0.2–0.3 V across a switch or relay contact, about 0.5 V for a whole side. A bigger number means resistance right there. No current, no valid test.
      3. Ground side. Black on battery −, red on the load's ground pin with the load on. More than about 0.3 V means a bad ground. One bad ground stud can kill several circuits, so look at what they share.
      4. Fuse keeps blowing. Battery off, fuse out, loads unplugged, switches open. Then Ω from the fuse output to battery −: near 0 Ω = a wire touching ground, OL = no short. With loads connected the test is weak, because a cold bulb or motor reads near 0 Ω too. A test light or short-finder in the fuse socket works too.
      5. Relays. Check for 12 V across pins 86 and 85 before blaming the relay. Most 12 V coils read roughly 50–150 Ω (check the relay's spec; a diode across the coil changes the reading). Good coil voltage but no click means a bad relay.
      6. Module inputs. Pull-up inputs sit at 5 V with the switch open and near 0 V with it closed. Pull-down inputs are the opposite. On a real truck, back-probe module pins with proper leads or a breakout box: never pierce the insulation or push probes into the terminals. The scan tool's live data shows what the module sees.
      7. Data links (CAN). Power off and modules asleep, Ω between CAN-H and CAN-L: 60 Ω = both 120 Ω terminators good, 120 Ω = one missing or the backbone open, about 40 Ω = an extra terminator, near 0 Ω = shorted together. Also check each wire to ground and to battery +. With traffic a meter reads CAN-H a bit above 2.5 V and CAN-L a bit below; a scope shows the real signal. This trainer has no traffic, so both sit at 2.5 V. J1587 is a different network with different rules.
